Helen lived quietly with her mother for the next fifteen years. Her brother Simeon, recalled to the home office, married and lived in New York. His mother and sister sometimes visited him and sometimes made little trips to the seashore and to other places where an elderly invalid could be comfortable.

When Helen was fifty her mother died, and Helen felt that she had lost everything. Simeon naturally did not feel like keeping up the old home now that his mother was dead; nor indeed did he feel that he could support Helen indefinitely now that she was free to work. Helen faced at fifty the problem of self-support.

Women of fifty sometimes marry, but usually these are widows. Helen had not acquired the marriage habit any more than she had acquired the business habit. After several conferences with Simeon it was decided that she should take boarders, Simeon generously deeding her his share of the old house and giving her a little money toward putting it in repair.

The consequence of Helen's life of self-abnegation was that every one spoke highly of her and pitied her.
